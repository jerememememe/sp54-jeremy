Same hand, different apparatus

You are about to step into five arrangements of equipment. Your hand is the same hand in each. What changes is the apparatus between your hand and the target: a rotation, a gain, a noise floor, a dropout rate, a drift.

The room

A panel of thirty-six squares floats in front of you. One lights amber. Steer the glowing point onto it and click. About a minute per arrangement, scored in bits per second, the measure Neuralink publishes for Arbaugh.

Before you enter

This does not simulate what Arbaugh feels, what spinal cord injury is like, or what it is like to have threads in the brain. It changes only the equipment around an ordinary hand.

You need a mouse or trackpad on a laptop or desktop. From the second arrangement onwards your pointer is captured, so the glowing point is all you see. Esc stops at any time. Nothing is recorded anywhere but this browser.

This device looks touch-only. The room needs a mouse or trackpad.

Block by block

One number, many worlds

Bits per second works as a boundary object (Star and Griesemer 1989). It stays recognisable across communities while meaning something different in each.

To an engineer it is a benchmark. To a company it is a headline. To a sceptic it asks what "high performance" means when the thing measured is a cursor. To Arbaugh it became a game he could play and win.

What did your number leave out? Fatigue, relief, the moment the habit broke. None of it fits in the score.
